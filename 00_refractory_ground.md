# Refractory Ground

## 1. Begin with what is happening

A person says something.

Another person understands it.

The sound disappears.

The understanding can remain.

The same thing can then be written down, repeated, translated, paraphrased, diagrammed, remembered, or taught to someone who was not present when the first person spoke.

The carriers change.

Something intelligible can survive those changes.

That is the starting observation.

No special theory is required to notice it.

---

## 2. Intelligibility is relational

A single mark does not become intelligible merely by existing.

A word is intelligible through relations: to other words, to a language, to a situation, to what has already been said, to what can follow.

A sentence is intelligible because its parts stand in an organization that can be grasped as a whole.

A theorem is intelligible because terms, conditions, quantities, operations, premises, and consequences stand in determinate relations.

A gesture is intelligible because it belongs to a situation.

A road sign is intelligible because the sign, the road, the driver, the convention, and the possible action bear on one another.

The claim here is not that every relation is intelligible.

The claim is that intelligibility requires relation.

If all relevant relations are removed, there is nothing by which one part can bear on another, nothing by which a difference can matter, and nothing by which a whole can be recovered.

So the first locked statement is:

> **Intelligibility requires relational organization.**

For the purposes of this sequence, we will use the shorter form:

> **Intelligibility is relational.**

---

## 3. Relation constrains

Once a relation is established, not every continuation is equivalent.

If I say:

> The key is inside the—

some continuations fit the established relation better than others.

If a proof has established a premise, later steps cannot ignore that premise without changing the proof.

If a promise has been made, later conduct is no longer unrelated to it.

If one object is defined as larger than another, a later statement that reverses the relation either changes the situation or contradicts what has already been established.

This is ordinary.

A relation makes a difference to what can coherently follow.

That is what is meant here by constraint.

Constraint does not mean compulsion in the physical sense.

It means that the space of possible continuation has acquired structure.

So the second locked statement is:

> **An established intelligible relation constrains its possible continuation.**

---

## 4. Constraint is the minimal basis for agency

The word *agency* often causes unnecessary confusion because it is immediately interpreted as conscious intention.

That is not the meaning being used here.

A minimal structural definition is enough:

> **Something has structural agency when it makes a difference to what can happen next.**

On that definition, an intelligible relation has agency.

Once established, it changes the possibilities of continuation.

It can be preserved, violated, completed, revised, contradicted, extended, or abandoned. But it cannot be treated as though it had made no difference at all while the same intelligible act is claimed to continue unchanged.

This does not imply that a sentence is conscious.

It does not imply that a theorem has desires.

It does not imply that abstract structures make choices.

It says only that intelligible relational organization is not inert with respect to its own continuation.

So the third locked statement is:

> **Intelligibility has structural agency because intelligible relations constrain what can coherently follow.**

If someone wishes to reserve the word *agency* for conscious beings, the underlying fact remains unchanged. The argument requires only effective constraint on continuation.

---

## 5. Completion is not punctuation

A written full stop can mark the end of a sentence.

It cannot make an unintelligible fragment complete merely by being placed after it.

Likewise, a sentence can be understood without written punctuation.

Speech existed before modern punctuation.

Questions can be recognized by structure and intonation.

A theorem can be expressed as an equation without ordinary sentence punctuation.

A text message can omit a final full stop and remain complete.

Punctuation is therefore a carrier-level device for indicating, assisting, or stabilizing a boundary.

It is not the fundamental cause of intelligible completion.

Completion occurs when enough relational organization has been achieved for the present act to stand as a whole.

More can still follow.

A completed sentence can be followed by another sentence.

A completed theorem can have consequences.

A completed repair can later fail.

Completion does not mean absolute finality.

It means sufficient completion relative to the demand that organized the act.

So the fourth locked statement is:

> **Intelligibility, not punctuation alone, determines whether a relational act has become sufficiently complete to stand as a whole.**

---

## 6. A sentence is an intelligible completion

In this sequence, *sentence* is used in the broad communicative sense:

> **a completed intelligible statement capable of standing for uptake.**

This includes ordinary grammatical sentences.

It also includes formal mathematical statements when they perform the same public function.

A theorem is therefore a sentence in this sense.

It can be stated.

It can be asserted.

It can be transmitted.

It can be understood.

It can be written in different notations and languages while remaining the same theorem in the relevant sense.

The purpose of saying this is not to erase the technical distinctions between natural-language grammar and formal logic.

It is to identify the shared phenomenon.

Both a theorem and an ordinary declarative sentence can function as completed, publicly transmissible intelligibility.

So:

> **Sentencehood at the level relevant here is a form of completed intelligibility, not merely a typographical shape.**

---

## 7. Carrier and intelligible structure are distinguishable

Consider the Pythagorean theorem.

It can be written in prose.

It can be written as an equation.

It can be represented diagrammatically.

It can be translated.

It can be spoken aloud.

It can be stored digitally.

It can be copied by someone who never met the previous copyist.

The marks, sounds, fonts, materials, languages, and speakers can change.

Yet another mind can still recover the same intelligible relation.

This gives a very simple distinction.

The physical or symbolic realization is the **carrier**.

The recoverable relational organization is the **intelligible structure**.

The two are not unrelated. A carrier is required for public transmission.

But they are not identical.

If they were identical, changing the carrier would necessarily destroy the intelligible identity.

It does not.

So:

> **Carrier identity is not identical with intelligible identity.**

This does not require the claim that intelligible structures float free of all carriers.

It requires only the much smaller claim that the same intelligible structure can be realized through more than one carrier.

---

## 8. Persistence is survival through replacement

Persistence does not have to mean remaining physically unchanged.

A spoken sentence persists if it is remembered and correctly repeated after the original sound waves are gone.

A theorem persists if later people can reconstruct it after the original manuscript is lost.

A rule persists if its force is carried into a new document.

A melody persists through different performances.

In each case, persistence occurs through replacement rather than through physical identity.

So the relevant notion is:

> **An intelligible structure persists when enough of its relational organization can be reconstructed through a new carrier.**

This is structural persistence across carriers.

It is one of the most ordinary facts of communication.

---

## 9. Communication is reconstruction, not transportation of a substance

When one person speaks to another, no object called *meaning* travels through the air as a substance.

Sound travels.

Marks travel.

Signals travel.

Another intelligence uses those carriers to reconstruct an intelligible organization.

The reconstruction can be accurate, partial, distorted, or unsuccessful.

This explains why communication can fail even when the carrier arrives perfectly.

A message can be heard but misunderstood.

A sentence can be copied exactly but interpreted wrongly.

A theorem can be memorized without being understood.

Successful communication therefore requires more than transfer of the carrier.

It requires recoverability of the relevant relational organization.

So:

> **Communication is the reconstruction of intelligible relational structure across a change of carrier.**

---

## 10. Transmission creates the problem of factorization

We can now state the problem that generates the rest of the sequence.

For an intelligible relation to become transmissible, it cannot merely occur.

It has to become sufficiently determinate to be reconstructed.

That immediately raises three different questions:

> **What changed?**

> **What made that change enough?**

> **How does the achieved result remain available?**

At this stage these are only questions.

No number of answers has been assumed.

No 6 × 3 × 3 architecture has been assumed.

No claim of exhaustiveness has been made.

But a crucial threshold has been crossed.

A completed act of intelligibility is no longer being treated as an indivisible lump.

It contains distinguishable structural work.

The remainder of the derivation asks whether these three forms of work are genuine factors, what recurring values they take, and what follows when those values are composed.

---

## Locked ground

The starting ground can now be stated in one sequence:

> Intelligibility is relational.

> Relation constrains possible continuation.

> Constraint is structural agency in the minimal sense that it makes a difference to what can follow.

> A relational act completes when it becomes sufficient to stand as a whole.

> Punctuation can mark completion but cannot create it by itself.

> A sentence is a publicly available form of completed intelligibility.

> The same intelligible structure can survive changes of carrier.

> Therefore carrier identity and intelligible identity are distinguishable.

> Persistence is the recoverability of relational organization through replacement of carrier.

> Communication is reconstruction of intelligible relational structure across carriers.

> Transmission therefore requires us to distinguish what changes, what makes the change enough, and how the achieved result remains available.

Nothing in this sequence depends on the 54.

That is precisely why it can serve as refractory ground.
