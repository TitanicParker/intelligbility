# The First Derivation: Why Transmission Requires Factorization

## Starting point

Intelligibility is relation.

This is the starting claim. It does not require a theory of language, a theory of mind, or a theory of mathematics. It says only that something becomes intelligible through relations: between parts, between events, between a sign and what it indicates, between a premise and a consequence, between one moment and another, between what is given and what is expected.

An isolated mark does not carry a complete intelligible structure by itself. A word gains intelligibility through its place in a language and in an utterance. A theorem gains intelligibility through relations among terms, conditions, implications, and proof. A gesture becomes intelligible through a situation. A sentence becomes intelligible through the organization of its parts into a whole.

Relation therefore comes before the present argument about grammar. The grammar is not being introduced in order to make relation true. The grammar is an attempt to describe what relational intelligibility must do when it becomes complete enough to be transmitted.

The next step is equally simple.

Relation constrains.

Once a relation is established, not every continuation preserves it equally. If a statement has begun in one way, some continuations fit it, some transform it, some contradict it, and some complete it. If a proof has established a premise, later steps are constrained by what has already been established. If a route has been opened, later movement is constrained by the route. If two things have been related, that relation changes what counts as a coherent next move.

Constraint is therefore not an additional theory placed on top of relation. It is what relation does. A relation makes a difference.

When enough relational structure has been established for a whole to stand, there is completion.

Completion does not mean that nothing more can ever be said. A completed sentence can be followed by another sentence. A completed theorem can have further consequences. A settled decision can later be revised. Completion means only that the present relational act no longer depends on further material in order to be intelligible as the whole it has become.

This gives us the first sequence:

**relation → constraint → completion**

But communication requires more.

A completed relation must be capable of becoming available beyond the particular event in which it first occurred. It must be speakable, writable, repeatable, rememberable, reconstructable, or otherwise capable of being taken up again.

That is persistence.

And once persistence is possible, transmission is possible.

So the fuller sequence is:

**relation → constraint → completion → persistence → transmission**

The question is now unavoidable:

**What different structural work has to be done for a relational process to become a completed, persistent, transmissible act of intelligibility?**

The answer begins with three functions that cannot simply be collapsed into one another:

1. something changes;
2. something makes that change enough;
3. something remains available after the change is complete.

These are not three labels for the same event. They answer three different questions.

---

## 1. Something must change

If nothing changes, nothing has been resolved.

This does not mean that every act of intelligibility must involve dramatic external change. The relevant change can be small. A distinction can become clear. A relation can be recognized. An ambiguity can be removed. A broken arrangement can be repaired. Separate elements can become integrated. An open question can become settled.

What matters is that an unresolved relational condition becomes differently organized.

Before the change, something remains incomplete, indeterminate, disconnected, unavailable, conflicted, or unsettled. After the change, the relation is not in the same condition.

This is the first function of completion:

**transformation**.

The word is not yet a claim about how many transformations exist. It names only the structural requirement that something about the unresolved relation must become otherwise.

Without transformation, there is no difference between before and after. Without a difference between before and after, there is no completed act to transmit.

So some account of **what changed** is unavoidable.

But knowing what changed does not tell us whether the change was enough.

A change can occur without completion.

A person can begin to repair a machine without finishing the repair. An argument can narrow disagreement without settling it. A sentence can develop without yet landing. A proof can advance without yet proving the theorem. A route can be partially opened without becoming usable.

Change is therefore necessary for completion, but change alone is not sufficient.

That forces a second question.

---

## 2. Something must make the change sufficient

A relational process can change continuously. If every change counted as completion, the distinction between development and completion would disappear.

So a completed act requires more than transformation. It requires a condition under which the transformation becomes **enough**.

That condition may be local or global. It may depend on one decisive point, on the organization of several parts, or on the removal of a dependency that had kept the process open. The exact forms can be investigated later. For the present argument, only the function matters.

Some condition must distinguish:

**the relation is still developing**

from:

**the relation now stands as complete enough to be taken up as a whole**.

This is the second function:

**completion condition**.

Again, the name does not impose a taxonomy. It identifies a structural job.

The distinction between transformation and completion condition can be seen directly.

The same kind of change can occur and yet count as complete under one condition and incomplete under another.

A repaired component may be sufficient if that component was the only fault. The same repair may be insufficient if the whole system still fails.

A definition may settle a local ambiguity while leaving the larger argument unresolved.

A vote may settle a decision in one institution because a majority is sufficient, while the same distribution of votes may fail to settle it in another institution because a supermajority is required.

The change can remain recognizably the same while the condition of enoughness differs.

Therefore:

**what changed** and **what made the change enough** are distinguishable.

They are not synonyms.

And because they can vary separately, they already exhibit the beginning of factorization.

But even a completed change is not yet transmission.

Something must remain.

---

## 3. Something must remain available

Suppose a relational process changes and reaches completion, but nothing of the achieved result remains available.

Then there is nothing to inherit.

The event may have happened, but it cannot function as communication, knowledge, instruction, memory, rule, theorem, agreement, or any other publicly recoverable achievement.

Transmission therefore requires a third function:

**persistence**.

Persistence does not mean that the original carrier must remain unchanged.

In fact, successful communication routinely demonstrates the opposite.

A spoken sentence disappears acoustically almost as soon as it is uttered, yet its intelligible structure may be remembered, repeated, paraphrased, translated, written down, or acted upon.

A theorem can survive the death of its speaker, the disappearance of its original manuscript, a change of alphabet, a change of notation, and a change of language.

What persists is not necessarily the physical event that first carried the intelligibility.

What persists is enough of the achieved relational organization for another act of reconstruction to occur.

This gives persistence its precise role:

**persistence is the mode by which a completed relational achievement remains available for subsequent uptake.**

This is different from transformation.

Two processes may arrive at the same enduring result by different transformations.

It is also different from completion condition.

Two acts may become complete under the same kind of sufficiency condition while leaving different kinds of availability behind.

A spoken warning and a written regulation can both settle what must be done, but they persist differently.

A repaired bridge and an agreed plan can both reach completion, yet what remains available is different in kind: one remains as an operative capability; the other as an articulated commitment.

The examples are not being offered as a final classification. They establish only the separability of the functions.

So the three questions are now:

**What changed?**

**What made that change enough?**

**How does the achieved result remain available?**

These questions are structurally different.

---

## Why this is factorization

Factorization does not initially mean a particular table, a particular number of values, or a claim of universal exhaustiveness.

At its most basic, factorization means that a phenomenon which appears as one completed whole contains distinguishable contributions that can vary without collapsing into one another.

That is exactly what has been established here.

A completed transmissible act of intelligibility is one event, but within that event at least three functions can be distinguished:

- the change that resolves the unresolved condition;
- the condition that makes the change sufficient;
- the mode by which the completed result remains available.

The claim becomes stronger when these functions can vary separately.

If the transformation changes while the completion condition and persistence remain recognizably stable, then transformation carries information not contained in the other two.

If the completion condition changes while the transformation and persistence remain stable, then completion condition carries information not contained in the other two.

If persistence changes while the transformation and completion condition remain stable, then persistence carries information not contained in the other two.

That is operational separability.

Operational separability is enough to justify speaking of factors.

It does not require absolute independence. Factors can interact. One factor can constrain the possible values of another. Some combinations can be common, others rare, and some impossible. A factorized structure does not have to be a perfectly unconstrained Cartesian product.

The essential point is simpler:

**the contribution of one factor is not exhausted by the contributions of the others.**

That is the threshold the argument must cross, and it does.

---

## The carrier cannot do this work by itself

It is important to head off an easy misunderstanding.

None of the three functions is reducible to punctuation, typography, wording, or physical medium.

A full stop may indicate that a sentence is complete, but a full stop can be placed after an incomplete fragment.

A page may preserve marks, but preserved marks are useless if the relational organization cannot be reconstructed.

A sequence of words may remain identical while its role changes with context.

A translated theorem can change almost every surface feature while preserving the relevant intelligible structure.

So the carrier participates in transmission, but it does not by itself determine the structure being transmitted.

The carrier is the vehicle through which reconstruction becomes possible.

The structure of intelligibility is what must be reconstructed.

That is why the three functions belong to relational intelligibility rather than to any particular medium.

---

## The three functions are not three meanings

Another confusion must be excluded.

The argument does not say that all meaning belongs to three categories.

Meaning can vary without apparent limit.

There can be indefinitely many things to say, prove, ask, imagine, promise, describe, calculate, remember, design, or dispute.

The factorization concerns something else.

It concerns the **structural work required for a meaning to become completed and transmissible**.

This is comparable to distinguishing the content of a journey from the structural requirements of travel. There may be indefinitely many destinations, but movement still requires distinguishable relations among departure, route, arrival, and continued access.

Likewise, there may be indefinitely many meanings while the structural conditions of completed transmission remain finite or recurrent.

The finitude of a grammar, if it is later established, would therefore not imply the finitude of meaning.

It would imply only that unlimited semantic variation can become publicly transmissible through a limited set of recurrent relational functions.

That is a different claim.

---

## What has been established, and what has not

At this point the argument has established a modest but consequential result:

**Completed transmissible intelligibility is not structurally featureless.**

For an unresolved relation to become transmissible, we must be able to distinguish:

1. the change by which the unresolved condition becomes otherwise;
2. the condition under which that change becomes sufficient;
3. the manner in which the completed achievement remains available.

These functions are conceptually distinct, and there are ordinary cases in which one varies while the others remain recognizably stable.

Therefore they are candidates for factors.

More strongly:

**if transmissible intelligibility requires these distinguishable functions, and if they can vary separately, then relational intelligibility is factorizable.**

Nothing further is needed for that conclusion.

What has **not** yet been established is equally important.

We have not established how many recurrent transformations exist.

We have not established how many forms of completion condition exist.

We have not established how many modes of persistence exist.

We have not established that one particular factorization is unique.

We have not established that every possible intelligible act must fit a finite atlas.

Those are subsequent questions.

The present derivation does not depend on them.

That is its strength.

The claim of factorization stands before any particular enumeration of factors.

---

## The result

We began with a simple statement:

**Intelligibility is relation.**

Relation constrains what can follow.

A relation can develop until it becomes sufficient to stand as a completed whole.

A completed relational achievement can remain available through a change of carrier.

That availability makes transmission and inheritance possible.

But transmission requires that three different structural questions be answerable:

**What changed?**

**What made that change enough?**

**How does the achieved result remain available?**

Because these questions perform different jobs, and because their answers can vary without simply collapsing into one another, the structure they describe is factorable.

So the first derivation can be stated plainly:

> **Transmissible intelligibility requires transformation, completion, and persistence to be distinguishable. Distinguishable contributions that can vary separately are factors. Therefore relational intelligibility is factorizable.**

This conclusion does not depend on punctuation.

It does not depend on a particular language.

It does not depend on a particular theorem.

It does not depend on one carrier.

It does not depend on any later claim about the number of forms.

It follows from the requirements of an intelligible relation becoming complete enough to survive its present carrier and become available for another act of understanding.

That is where the grammar properly begins.
