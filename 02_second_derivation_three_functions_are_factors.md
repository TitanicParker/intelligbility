# The Second Derivation: Why the Three Functions Are Genuine Factors

## The remaining escape

The first derivation established three distinguishable functions inside transmissible intelligibility:

1. something changes;
2. something makes that change sufficient;
3. something makes the achieved result remain available.

A reasonable objection remains:

> Perhaps these are only three descriptions of one underlying event.

If that were true, calling them *factors* would be premature.

So this document has one job:

> **show that transformation, completion, and persistence can vary separately enough that none can be reduced to either of the others.**

This is the threshold between a three-part description and a factorized structure.

---

## 1. What a factor means here

The word *factor* does not mean complete independence.

It does not mean that each factor can take any value regardless of the others.

It does not mean that the factors never interact.

It means something more modest and more useful:

> **A factor is a distinguishable contribution to a whole that carries information not already supplied by the other contributions.**

A practical test follows.

If we can hold two contributions recognizably stable while varying the third, then the third is doing work that the other two do not determine.

That is operational separability.

Operational separability is enough for factorization.

---

## 2. Transformation does not determine completion

Consider two repairs.

In both cases a loose electrical connection is tightened.

The same kind of change occurs: a fault in the connection is repaired.

In the first device, that loose connection was the only fault. Once tightened, the device works.

In the second device, the connection is tightened in exactly the same way, but a second failure remains elsewhere. The device still does not work.

The transformation is recognizably the same.

The condition under which the repair counts as enough is different.

So:

> **what changed does not by itself determine whether the change is sufficient.**

Transformation and completion condition are therefore not the same contribution.

The same logic appears in ordinary reasoning.

A distinction may be successfully made but still fail to settle the larger question.

A route may be opened but still fail to reach the required destination.

A relation may be established but remain too local to organize the whole.

A disagreement may narrow without becoming settled.

In each case, real change occurs before completion.

Therefore completion cannot be reduced to change.

---

## 3. Completion does not determine transformation

Now reverse the comparison.

Two situations both become complete at one decisive point.

In the first, the decisive point is identifying which of two wires is live.

In the second, the decisive point is choosing which of two proposals will be adopted.

Both complete focally around one decisive locus.

But the transformations are different.

The first completes by making a difference operative.

The second completes by discharging a live demand for choice.

So:

> **the same structural condition of enoughness can accompany different kinds of change.**

Completion condition therefore does not determine transformation.

The fact that a process completes around one decisive locus tells us something important about how it becomes sufficient.

It does not tell us what kind of unresolvedness was transformed.

---

## 4. Transformation does not determine persistence

Consider two cases in which an ambiguity is resolved.

In the first, the result remains as a written definition.

In the second, the result remains only as a practical ability to distinguish two cases correctly during operation.

The transformation in both cases is differentiation: an operative difference becomes available.

But what remains available after completion differs.

In one case the achieved determination stands explicitly.

In the other, the result persists primarily as an effective capacity.

So:

> **the same transformation can leave different kinds of persistent result.**

Transformation therefore does not determine persistence.

---

## 5. Persistence does not determine transformation

Reverse the comparison again.

Two results both remain as standing rules.

One rule arose by resolving an ambiguity.

The other arose by settling a dispute.

Both persist as explicit determinations that can later be consulted.

Yet the transformation that produced each result differs.

The first makes a difference operative.

The second discharges a demand for adjudication.

So:

> **the same mode of persistence can result from different transformations.**

Persistence therefore does not determine transformation.

---

## 6. Completion does not determine persistence

Consider two completed decisions.

Both become sufficient when a final authority selects one option.

The completion condition is the same kind of focal settlement.

But the first decision remains as a written policy.

The second remains as an operative permission: people can now act in a way that was previously blocked.

Both are complete.

What remains available differs.

So:

> **the condition that made an act complete does not by itself determine how the completed result persists.**

Completion and persistence are not the same contribution.

---

## 7. Persistence does not determine completion

Now take two results that both persist as operative capacities.

In one case a machine becomes usable because one decisive component is repaired.

In another, a team becomes functional only when several roles become coordinated across the group.

Both results persist as efficacy: something can now be done.

But one completion is focal.

The other is distributed.

So:

> **the same mode of persistence can arise under different conditions of sufficiency.**

Persistence therefore does not determine completion.

---

## 8. The six reciprocal tests

We can now display the full separability pattern.

1. Same transformation, different completion.
2. Same completion, different transformation.
3. Same transformation, different persistence.
4. Same persistence, different transformation.
5. Same completion, different persistence.
6. Same persistence, different completion.

Every pair can come apart.

That matters.

If the three functions were merely three synonyms for one property, these contrasts would be impossible or trivial.

Instead, each function answers a different structural question and can vary while the others remain recognizably stable.

This is exactly what factorization requires.

---

## 9. Why “different descriptions” is not enough

A critic might still say:

> Of course one event can be described in several ways. That does not prove factors.

Correct.

Multiple descriptions alone do not establish factorization.

The stronger evidence is **crossed variation**.

Suppose one variable were merely a redescription of another.

Then fixing the first should substantially fix the second.

But that does not happen here.

Knowing that the transformation is a repair does not tell us whether one locus is enough, whether a whole distributed system must be restored, or whether completion occurs by release from a prior governing fault.

Knowing that completion is focal does not tell us whether the event is a differentiation, opening, relation, repair, integration, or settlement.

Knowing that the result persists as efficacy does not tell us what changed or what made that change sufficient.

Each contribution therefore carries information the others do not.

That is not merely descriptive plurality.

It is non-redundant structure.

---

## 10. Factorization does not require full independence

Another objection should be blocked early.

A factorized system can contain interactions.

For example, some transformations may naturally favor some completion conditions.

Some combinations may be easy to realize.

Others may be rare.

Some may require more context.

A factor can constrain another factor without ceasing to be a factor.

The relevant question is not:

> Can every value combine equally freely with every other value?

The relevant question is:

> Does each factor make a distinguishable contribution that cannot be recovered from the others alone?

If yes, the system is factorized even if its combinations have unequal widths or probabilities.

This is common in many structured systems.

The dimensions of a physical object can interact through constraints and still be dimensions.

The grammatical roles in a language can constrain one another and still be distinguishable roles.

The ingredients of a chord interact and still contribute separately.

Factorization is compatible with structured dependence.

---

## 11. Factorization does not require metaphysical separateness

A second unnecessary demand is metaphysical isolation.

No claim is being made that transformation, completion, and persistence exist as three independent substances.

They are functions within one act.

They belong together.

Indeed, the point of the grammar is that a completed act of intelligibility is generated by their conjunction.

A melody has pitch, rhythm, and duration without those being three separate melodies.

A journey has movement, arrival condition, and continuing accessibility without those being three separate journeys.

Likewise, transmissible intelligibility can have distinguishable factors while remaining one event.

So:

> **factorization is internal articulation, not ontological disassembly.**

---

## 12. The minimal theorem of separability

The argument can now be stated in compact form.

Let a completed transmissible act contain three distinguishable contributions:

- \(T\): what changes;
- \(C\): what makes the change sufficient;
- \(P\): how the achieved result remains available.

If:

- \(T\) can vary while \(C\) and \(P\) remain recognizably stable;
- \(C\) can vary while \(T\) and \(P\) remain recognizably stable;
- \(P\) can vary while \(T\) and \(C\) remain recognizably stable;

then none of \(T\), \(C\), or \(P\) is fully determined by the other two.

Therefore each carries non-redundant structural information.

Therefore they function as factors.

No particular values have yet been assumed.

---

## 13. Why this matters for transmission

The factorization is not being imposed for convenience.

It follows from what reconstruction requires.

Another intelligence trying to inherit an intelligible act may need to recover:

- what happened;
- why that happening counts as enough;
- what is now available as a result.

Confusing those questions produces predictable errors.

A person may understand what changed but not realize that the matter remains unresolved.

A person may understand that something is complete but misunderstand what exactly changed.

A person may understand the completed result but fail to understand whether it stands as a determination, depends on a relation, or persists as a capacity or consequence.

Those are different failures of reconstruction.

That is further evidence that the functions are not interchangeable.

---

## 14. The conclusion

The first derivation showed that transmission requires three distinguishable forms of structural work.

The second derivation has now shown that those forms of work can vary separately.

Therefore they are not merely three perspectives on one undifferentiated event.

They are factors.

The result can be stated plainly:

> **A completed transmissible act of intelligibility contains at least three non-redundant structural contributions: transformation, completion, and persistence. Because each contribution can vary while the others remain recognizably stable, relational intelligibility is factorizable.**

This conclusion still does not require:

- six Transformation Patterns;
- three Completion Topologies;
- three Persistence Modes;
- fifty-four positions;
- exhaustiveness;
- uniqueness;
- universality.

Those are later questions.

What is now established is the ground they would have to occupy.

The next task is no longer to ask whether factors exist.

It is to ask:

> **What recurrent values do these factors actually take?**
