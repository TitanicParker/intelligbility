Intelligibility is relation.

Something is intelligible because its parts, features, events, or terms stand in relations that can be grasped together. Intelligibility is therefore not located in an isolated mark, sound, word, symbol, object, or event. It arises through the organization of relations among them.

Relation constrains what can follow.

Once a relation has been established, not every continuation preserves it equally. Some continuations fit it, some change it, some contradict it, some complete it, and some destroy it. A relation therefore makes a difference to the possibilities that follow from it.

That constraint is agency in the minimal structural sense used here.

Agency does not have to mean conscious intention. It means that something is capable of making a difference to what can happen next. An intelligible relation has this capacity because, once established, it constrains its possible continuation.

When a relation becomes sufficient, it completes.

Completion does not mean that nothing more can ever be added. It means that enough relational organization has been achieved for the whole to stand intelligibly without requiring further material in order to be grasped as that whole.

A sentence is one ordinary example of this.

A sentence is not made a sentence by punctuation alone. Punctuation can indicate a boundary, but it cannot create intelligibility where there is none. A full stop can be placed after an incomplete or unintelligible expression, and an intelligible sentence can be recognized without a written full stop.

Sentence completion is therefore determined more fundamentally by intelligibility than by punctuation.

The carrier and the intelligibility carried by it are not the same thing.

A sentence may be spoken or written. It may be translated. Its typography may change. Its exact words may sometimes change through paraphrase while the relevant intelligible relation remains recoverable.

The physical or symbolic form through which intelligibility is made available is the carrier.

A theorem provides a particularly clear example.

A theorem can be stated as a sentence. Its wording, notation, language, diagram, script, speaker, and physical medium can change while the same theorem remains recognizable and recoverable.

The Pythagorean theorem can be spoken, written as an equation, written as prose, translated into another language, represented in a diagram, printed in a book, stored digitally, or taught by one person to another.

The carriers differ.

The intelligible relational structure can persist.

Therefore the identity of an intelligible structure cannot be identical with the identity of any one of its carriers.

When a completed intelligible relation can be reconstructed through another carrier, it persists.

Persistence here does not require a relation to exist without any carrier whatsoever. It requires only that the relational organization can survive the replacement of one carrier by another.

This is what makes inheritance possible.

One mind can produce a carrier from which another mind reconstructs an intelligible relation. The second mind does not receive the first mind, the original sound waves, or the original physical marks. It receives a new carrier through which sufficiently corresponding relational organization becomes available.

Communication is therefore the inheritance of intelligible relational structure across carriers.

This does not mean that communication always succeeds perfectly. Relations can be lost, distorted, misunderstood, or only partly recovered. But successful communication requires enough relational structure to persist through the change of carrier for another intelligence to reconstruct what matters.

Meaning may vary without apparent limit.

What can be meant is not therefore being claimed to form a small finite inventory.

The question is different.

What structural work must be accomplished for any meaning to become sufficiently complete, transmissible, reconstructable, and available to another intelligence?

At minimum, three different questions can be asked of such an event.

What changes in the relation?

What makes that change sufficient as a completion?

How does the achieved result remain available?

These are not three meanings.

They are not three subjects.

They are not three kinds of sentence.

They are three distinguishable functions within the achievement of transmissible intelligibility.

If those functions can vary while remaining distinguishable, then relational intelligibility has internal structure that can be separated into factors.

That is what is meant here by factorization.

Factorization does not yet mean that one particular factorization is uniquely correct.

It does not yet establish that there must be exactly six values of one factor, three of another, and three of another.

It does not yet establish that every possible act of intelligibility belongs to a particular finite atlas.

Those are later questions.

The prior claim is simpler:

Relational intelligibility can contain distinguishable contributions that perform different structural functions.

If those contributions recur, can vary, and can be recombined while remaining recognizable, relational intelligibility is factorizable.

The grammar begins from that fact.

The carrier may change.

The relational structure may persist.

What persists can be inherited.

What can be inherited must become sufficiently determinate to be reconstructed.

The structure of that determination is the object of the grammar.
